TL;DR
A physical card reader (mPOS) had a 30% payment acceptance rate and an NPS of -10. The root cause wasn't UX or a technical bug — it was a fraud-prevention rule silently penalizing legitimate merchants grouped with fraudulent ones. I redesigned the merchant risk model and the decline-screen UX, renegotiated an unrealistic OKR using data, and took acceptance rate to 75% and NPS to +40pp in 6 months.
📋 Context & Problem — starting point
▾
Product context

mPOS is the physical card reader. It connects via Bluetooth to the merchant's phone and accepts any card via contactless, chip or magstripe. Funds from every sale settle directly to the merchant's account.

The problem

Taking over the product, the initial diagnosis revealed two critical, interrelated problems:

📉
Acceptance rate: 30%
70% of payment attempts ended in decline. The reasons weren't well categorized or understood.
😤
NPS: -10
Top detractors: "Connectivity issues," "Payment declined," "Took longer than expected."
🔍 Discovery: three parallel tracks — how I investigated it
▾

The process wasn't linear. We worked simultaneously across three tracks that fed into each other. Tracks A and B were quick wins for fast impact while we investigated the root cause (Track C).

A
Connectivity & latency — technical analysis with Engineering
  • Reviewed BLE logs and analyzed timeouts with the Engineering team
  • The timeout was triggering too fast — before the transaction completed
  • The pairing walkthrough didn't communicate the optimal distance between phone and reader
→ Part of the problem was avoidable with better onboarding guidance.
B
Decline-screen UX — redesigning the messaging
  • Reviewed every post-decline error screen
  • No distinction existed between final and recoverable declines
  • Final declines still showed a retry CTA — multiplying frustration
→ Final declines had no "don't retry" messaging, multiplying user frustration.
C
Acceptance rate by MCC — the central finding
  • Broke down acceptance rate by merchant and MCC code
  • Worst performers: motorcycle couriers, event-space rentals, high-ticket services
  • All shared the same code: a generic "Decline 05" masking multiple fraud rules
  • Convened a technical session with Visa to decode it
→ Fraudulent merchants were contaminating the acceptance rate of legitimate merchants in the same category.
💡 Turning-point insight
The problem wasn't technical or UX — it was structural and risk-based. Fraud vectors flagged by Visa were penalizing an entire merchant category, including completely legitimate businesses. Fixing acceptance rate required redesigning how merchants were categorized.
⚙️ Solutions — what we built
▾
1
Technical fixes + redesigned connection onboarding
Fixed BLE bugs, optimized timeouts, and redesigned the pairing walkthrough with clear visual guidance on the required distance. Added connectivity error messages with actionable next steps.
2
Differentiated decline experiences by type
Final declines (funds, fraud, blocked card): explicit no-retry messaging, no retry CTA.
Recoverable declines (error, timeout): retry CTA available, empathetic resolution-oriented messaging.
3
Merchant risk-scoring system (anti-fraud)
Together with the Onboarding team, designed a scoring system to classify each merchant and assign them to a risk-based category:

TL;DR
Promotion terms were negotiated with brands and processors, but loaded manually — Business defined rules in a spreadsheet, Operations turned them into tickets, Engineering hard-coded them into production. The real bottleneck wasn't Operations' capacity: Business had no direct way to express its own rules. I built a guided, self-service tool with an automatic prioritization engine, cutting load time from 48 hours to 30 minutes and manual errors by 90%.
📋 Context & Problem — starting point
▾

The company closed promotion agreements (interest-free installments, cashback, etc.) with different brands and processors. The problem: many promotions overlapped, with no systematic logic to prioritize them. The flow was fully manual — Business defined the rules in a spreadsheet, Operations generated a ticket, and Engineering loaded them directly into code.

⚠️ The real cost of the process
Promotions closing same-day forced Engineering to step in after hours. Any spreadsheet error — wrong cashback %, wrong dates — went straight into production issues.
🔍 Discovery: two tracks, one root cause — how I investigated it
▾

I led a discovery process with research, benchmarking, and interviews across the two teams living the problem: Operations and Business. What I found in each one changed the direction of the design.

A
Mapping the process with Operations
  • Interviewed the team translating spreadsheets into tickets for Engineering
  • Every promotion: receive spreadsheet → interpret it → ticket → wait for Engineering
  • Last-minute promotions forced after-hours work
  • Any ambiguity in the spreadsheet meant another round of back-and-forth
→ Operations was the visible bottleneck — but it was a symptom, not the cause.
B
Interviews with Business: the missing voice in the system
  • Interviewed the team closing promotion deals
  • They had no direct channel into the system — everything routed through two intermediary teams
  • Prioritization between overlapping promotions was decided informally
  • Result: frequent inconsistencies and full dependence on Engineering for any change
→ The real bottleneck was access, not capacity. Business had no voice in the system.
💡 Turning-point insight
The problem wasn't that Operations was slow — it was that Business had no way to express its rules without going through two intermediary teams. That reframed the whole design: the solution had to start from Business, not from Operations.
⚙️ Solution — what we built
▾
1
Self-service interface for Business
Built a loading capability designed as a guided walkthrough for the Business team, removing the need for intermediaries to define and load a promotion.

Automatic prioritization engine

📈 Key decision, Impact & Learnings — outcome and reflection
▾
Key decision
📌 Design for the real user, not the technical one
The instinct was to build a tool for Operations. Interviews revealed the real bottleneck was upstream: Business had no way to express its rules without two intermediary teams. Starting the workflow from Business was the decision that unlocked the whole flow.
Business impact

The tool let Business close and activate promotions in real time — with no operational friction or dependency on Engineering. This unblocked new promotions that otherwise couldn't have been implemented.

🔗 Direct input for a real-time payments initiative
The transaction volume generated by merchants from the newly activated promotions became direct input to scale and push a related real-time payments feature ("Pago Inmediato") — connecting operational impact to a larger strategic initiative.
Learnings
The slowest process always has an owner Before designing the solution, discovery revealed the problem wasn't technical — it was access. Business had no voice in the system. Finding that human bottleneck was more valuable than any technical fix.
Operational efficiency has strategic impact Reducing internal friction isn't just a process improvement — in this case it enabled a go-to-market speed that translated into new promotions, more transactions, and a new product line.
